{#abstract1-anchor}ABSTRACT {#abstract1}
----------------------------------------

Interleukins (ILs) are pleiotropic mediators of immune signaling and represent promising targets for antiviral intervention. Among them, IL‐19 is upregulated in response to viral infections, yet its functional significance and mechanistic basis have been poorly defined. This study identifies an unforeseen antiviral activity of IL‐19. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that IL‐19 signals through the IL‐20RB receptor to drive STAT3 phosphorylation, and this activated IL‐20RB/STAT3 pathway then induces TRIM22 expression, culminating in enhanced IFN‐λ secretion and the direct suppression of viral replication. Importantly, despite significantly curtailing HSV‐1 replication in murine brain and lung tissues, IL‐19 administration failed to confer a survival benefit, an effect attributable to aggravated pulmonary inflammation. These findings clarify IL‐19 as a dual‐function cytokine with both protective antiviral and detrimental proinflammatory properties, illustrating the intricate immunomodulatory functions of cytokines during viral infections.
**Keywords:** innate immunity, interferon, interleukin‐19, STAT3, TRIM22

{#jmv71066-sec-0010-anchor}1. Introduction {#jmv71066-sec-0010}
---------------------------------------------------------------

Emerging and re‐emerging viral infectious diseases (e.g., Chikungunya fever, SARS‐CoV‐2, Ebola, Zika) pose significant global health and economic burdens \[[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0001), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0002)\]. After invading the host, viruses cause various outcomes ranging from asymptomatic infection to fatal disease. In response to viral infections, our bodies have evolved a sophisticated immune system \[[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0003), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0004)\]. Among them, innate immunity---the first line of defense---detects pathogens via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), triggering interferon (IFN) and cytokine production to combat infection \[[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0005), [6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0006)\]. Type I/III IFNs exert antiviral effects by inducing interferon‐stimulated genes (ISGs) through JAK‐STAT signaling \[[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0007), [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0008)\].

Interleukins (ILs) are among the earliest characterized cytokines. To date, more than 41 classes of ILs have been identified \[[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0009)\], implying a meticulously modulated signaling pathway of the host's immune system. Previous studies have demonstrated that there is an intricate interplay between ILs and IFNs. For example, IL‐1β activates cGAS‐STING pathway by promoting the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), thereby directly triggering a potent innate immune response to restrict dengue virus infection \[[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0010)\]. IL‐3 can increase the secretion of CXCL12, and then promote the plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC) to secret IFN‐α and IFN‐λ, thus fight against HSV‐1 infection \[[11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0011)\]. IL‐27 can restrict HBV replication by up‐regulating TRIM25 \[[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0012)\], and also plays an anti‐HIV function by inducing posttranslational modification of Y box binding protein‐1 (YB‐1) in CD4+ T cells \[[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0013)\]. IL‐36 acts as an antiviral cytokine by enhancing the expression of the type I IFN receptor and thus potentiating IFN signaling \[[14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0014)\]. Together, these examples demonstrate that diverse IL family members can exert antiviral effects. As a result, IL‐targeted strategies offer a promising avenue for antiviral interventions.

IL‐19, an IL‐10 family member, and specifically IL‐20 subfamily (including IL‐20, IL‐22, IL‐24, IL‐26 as well) \[[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0015)\], is secreted by immune cells (monocytes, macrophages, B cells) and stromal cells (keratinocytes, fibroblasts, airway epithelia) \[[16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0016)\]. It plays functions through a heterodimeric receptor‐ligand complex: IL‐19 binds to IL‐20 receptor β (IL‐20RB) on the surface of target cells, and then recruits IL‐20 receptor α (IL‐20RA) to form the IL‐20RB/IL‐20RA/IL‐19 complex \[[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0017)\]. The signal transduction of IL‐19 is mainly mediated by phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription‐3 (STAT3) \[[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0018)\], which requires IL‐20RB but not IL‐20RA \[[19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0019)\]. Interestingly, IL‐19 can also activate the phosphorylation of extracellular signal‐regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) via NF‐κB signaling pathway and then promote the expression of nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2) through IL‐20RA \[[20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0020), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0021)\]. IL‐19 has been found to contribute to diverse pathology including cardiovascular disease, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, and rheumatoid arthritis \[[22](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0022)\]. Earlier studies have identified associations between IL‐19 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and susceptibility to respiratory syncytial virus \[[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0023)\], HBV \[[24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0024)\], and HIV infections \[[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0025)\]. Notably, clinical evidence has linked IL‐19 levels to the outcomes of COVID‐19 patients. Serum IL‐19 levels are significantly elevated in severe COVID‐19 patients versus mild cases and healthy controls \[[26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0026), [27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0027)\]. In addition, pregnant COVID‐19 patients also show upregulated plasma IL‐19 levels \[[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0028)\]. Collectively, these studies suggest that IL‐19 can play multiple roles by modulating host immunity through complex signaling networks, warranting further mechanistic investigation. So far, its function in antiviral immunity remains unknown. In the present study, we identify a novel role of IL‐19‐mediated antiviral immunity, and further clarify the underlying mechanism that IL‐19 activates an IL‐20RB/STAT3‐dependent pathway to upregulate TRIM22 and IFN‐λ, thereby inhibiting viral replication.

{#jmv71066-sec-0020-anchor}2. Materials and Methods {#jmv71066-sec-0020}
------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.1. Cell Lines {#jmv71066-sec-0030}

A549 cells (from human alveolar adenocarcinoma basal epithelial cells, ATCC, CCL‐185), 293T cells (from the embryonic kidney of a female human fetus, ATCC, CRL‐3216), and Vero cells (from the kidney of a female normal adult African green monkey, ATCC, CCL‐81) were maintained in complete Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), at 37°C with 5% CO2. BMM cells (from mouse bone‐marrow‐derived macrophage) were cultured in conditioned RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.8), and 1% M‐CSF (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA).

### 2.2. Viruses {#jmv71066-sec-0040}

HSV‐GFP‐Luci F strain (HSV‐1) was stored in our lab. The pathogenic HSV‐1 McKrae strain was gifted by Prof. Jumin Zhou (Kunming Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Kunming, China), which was used in the animal study.

### 2.3. Virus Preparation {#jmv71066-sec-0050}

Vero cells at over 90% confluency were infected with HSV at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI = 0.01) and cultured in serum‐free or low‐serum (2% FBS) medium containing 1% penicillin‐streptomycin for approximately 3 days. After the complete cytopathic effect was observed in all cells, both cells and supernatant were harvested. The viral suspension underwent three freeze‐thaw cycles, followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane and aliquoted for storage at −80°C.

### 2.4. TCID50 {#jmv71066-sec-0060}

The virus titer was determined by the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50). Vero cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 10^4^ cells/well. After 24 h, virus‐containing fluid was serially diluted to 10^−1^ to 10^−8^ fold using DMEM containing 2% FBS \[[29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0029)\]. Then, 100 µL per well of the dilution was added to a 96‐well plate. The 96‐well plate was incubated at 37°C and 5% CO~2~ atmosphere, and the viral cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed every 24 h. The TCID50 was calculated using the Karber formula.

### 2.5. Plasmid and siRNA Transfection {#jmv71066-sec-0070}

Different plasmids (pcDNA3.1‐IL‐19(HA‐tag), pcDNA3.1‐TRIM22(HA‐tag), pc DNA3.1‐IL20RB(Flag‐tag) and pcDNA3.1‐Empty) and siRNA (IL20RB‐homo‐376 and IL20RB‐homo‐423) were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's manuals. The medium was replaced with DMEM containing 5% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin after 6 h transfection, and then the cells were incubated for 24 or 48 h. The sequences of siRNA are described in Supporting Information S2: Table [S1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-suppl-0002).

### 2.6. Quantitative Real‐Time PCR {#jmv71066-sec-0080}

The total RNA was extracted by using an RNA extraction kit (EZBioscience, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's manual. The cDNA was synthesized with Hifair III 1st strand cDNA (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and mixed with PerfectStart SYBR Green SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China), followed by quantification of gene expression with ABI Quanstudio 7 Flex system (Applied Biosystems). The primer sequences used in our study, including housekeeping genes (GAPDH for human cells, β‐actin for mouse tissues), are described in Supporting Information S2: Table [S2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-suppl-0002).

### 2.7. Luciferase Assay {#jmv71066-sec-0090}

The Steady‐Glo Renilla Luciferase detection system and Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay were employed as we previously described \[[30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0030)\], according to the manufacturer's instructions (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

### 2.8. Western Blotting Analysis {#jmv71066-sec-0100}

The Western blotting assay was performed as we previously described \[[31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0031)\]. Antibodies against the GAPDH(M20006L), HA‐Tag (M20003L), Phospho‐STAT3 ([T56566](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/T56566)), and STAT3([T55292](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/T55292)) were purchased from Abmart (Shanghai, China). Antibodies against IL‐19 (ab154187) and IL‐20RB (ab281996) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Antibody against UL27(H1142) was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA).

### 2.9. RNA‐Seq Library Preparation, Sequencing, and Data Processing {#jmv71066-sec-0110}

The RNA‐seq assay was performed as we previously described \[[32](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0032)\]. In brief, A549 cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1‐IL19 or pcDNA3.1‐empty at a dose of 2 µg for 24 h. Then, the cells were infected with HSV‐1 (MOI = 0.5) for 12 h. The samples were collected for the total RNA and then used to generate RNA‐seq libraries with the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v4‐cBot‐HS (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The raw data obtained by Illumina HiSeq sequencing were filtered, and the clean reads were aligned to the reference sequence. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq. 2 (version 1.38.0). Genes with an adjusted *p* value (Benjamini--Hochberg false discovery rate, FDR) \< 0.05 and \|log~2~(fold change)\| \> 1 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was performed by using the KEGG database ([http://www.kegg.jp](http://www.kegg.jp/)), and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed by using the GO knowledgebase (<https://geneontology.org/>).

### 2.10. Animals {#jmv71066-sec-0120}

WT C57BL/6 female mice, which were 6 \~ 8 weeks from Lingfu Tuopu Biotechnology, were used in this study. All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Boards and Animal Care and Use Committees of Lingfu Tuopu Biotechnology (Approval Number: TOPGM‐IACUC‐2024‐0035). Mice were bred in an animal facility where: (1) air exchange rate of up to 30 times per hour; (2) a 12 h light/dark rhythm; (3) air humidity is between 45% and 65%; (4) the temperature is between 20°C and 24°C; and the (5) the animals receive autoclaved water.

### 2.11. IL‐19 Treatment {#jmv71066-sec-0130}

Mice of the IL‐19 group were administered daily intraperitoneal injections of mIL‐19(GLPBIO, CA, USA) at a dose of 10 ng/g in a volume of 100 μL PBS from 3 days before infection to 1 day after infection (for 5 consecutive days). Mice of the PBS group were administered 100 μL PBS in the same way.

### 2.12. Mice Infection {#jmv71066-sec-0140}

Three days after IL‐19 administration, mice were placed in a sealed chamber containing isoflurane for 10 s to induce anesthesia. Except for the mock group, mice were anesthetized \[[11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0011)\] and infected intranasally with 50 μL (1 × 10^5^ PFU) of HSV‐1. The disease symptoms and weight change of mice were monitored daily. Mice that lost greater than 30% of their initial body weight were immediately sacrificed as per protocol \[[33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0033)\].

### 2.13. Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay {#jmv71066-sec-0150}

Mouse serum was collected on Day 6 after infection and detected by the mouse IL‐19 ELISA Kit (Meimian, Jiangsu, China), and the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected on Day 8 after infection and detected by the mouse IFN‐λ ELISA Kit (Meimian, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

### 2.14. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H\&E) Staining {#jmv71066-sec-0160}

The different tissues of experimental mice were immersed completely in 4% paraformaldehyde, gradually dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and cut into sections. H\&E staining was performed according to standard protocol by Wuhan Service Biotechnology, Wuhan, China.

### 2.15. Statistical Analysis {#jmv71066-sec-0170}

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 8 (GraphPad Software Inc.). The Student's *t* ‐test was performed to compare differences between two groups. One‐way ANOVA was used to determine statistically significant differences between three groups. \**p* \< 0.05; \*\**p* \< 0.01; \*\*\**p* \< 0.001; \*\*\*\**p* \< 0.0001.

{#jmv71066-sec-0180-anchor}3. Results {#jmv71066-sec-0180}
----------------------------------------------------------

### 3.1. Antiviral Activities of IL‐19 {#jmv71066-sec-0190}

To comprehensively characterize IL‐19 expression during viral infections, we conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis comparing IL‐19 levels between COVID‐19 patients and healthy controls (search strategy detailed in Supporting Information S1: Figure [S1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-suppl-0001)). The IL‐19 level in COVID‐19 patients is 2.52 standard units (95% CI: 1.47--3.57) higher than that in healthy individuals across included clinical datasets (Figure [1A](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0001)). Given that viral infections activate innate immunity via Toll‐like receptors (TLRs) \[[34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0034)\], we stimulated bone marrow‐derived macrophage (BMM) cells with TLR agonists, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and Polyinosinic‐polycytidylic acid (Poly I:C). Both treatments significantly induced IL‐19 expression in a time‐dependent manner (Figure [1B,C](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0001)). Furthermore, infection of BMM and A549 cells with HSV‐1 also resulted in enhanced IL‐19 expression over time (Figure [1D,E](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0001)). Corroborating these in vitro findings, serum IL‐19 levels were significantly elevated in HSV‐1‐infected C57BL/6 mice compared to uninfected controls (Figure [1F](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0001)). Together, these data demonstrate that viral infections robustly induce the host's IL‐19 expression.

To assess the biological function of IL‐19 in viral infections, we transfected A549 cells with increasing amounts of IL‐19‐expressing plasmid for 24 h, followed by infection with HSV‐1 for 12 h. qPCR analysis revealed that IL‐19 overexpression significantly inhibited HSV‐1 replication (Figure [1G](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0001)), and this result was further confirmed by Western Blotting analysis (Figure [1H](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0001)). To determine the kinetics of IL‐19's antiviral activity, we measured viral replication at 4, 8, and 12 h post‐HSV‐1 infection. The results demonstrated that IL‐19 exerted significant antiviral effects, with its inhibitory activity strengthening over time (Figure [1I](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0001)).

### 3.2. IL‐19 Modulates Innate Immunity via the IL‐20RB/STAT3 Pathway {#jmv71066-sec-0200}

To further investigate IL‐19‐mediated signaling pathways underlying its immunomodulatory function, we performed RNA‐seq in IL‐19‐overexpressing versus control cells. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed enrichment in cytokine--cytokine receptor interactions and JAK‐STAT signaling, alongside pathways associated with viral infections (Figure [2A](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0002)). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis further implicated IL‐19's function in regulating viral genome replication and host cell entry (Figure [2B](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0002)). Given that IL‐19 specifically activates cytokine‐receptor interactions, we hypothesized that IL‐19 requires IL‐20RB for its antiviral function, since STAT3 phosphorylation---the canonical signaling event downstream of IL‐19---is mediated by IL‐20RB but not IL‐20RA \[[19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0019)\]. Interestingly, we found that incubation with recombinant IL‐19 protein exerted antiviral effects in A549 but not in 293 T cells (Figure [2C,D](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0002)), with no acute cytotoxicity (Supporting Information S1: Figure [S2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-suppl-0001)). Consistently, transfection with IL‐19‐expressing plasmid also showed no antiviral activity in 293T cells (Figure [3E,F](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0003)). Further analysis demonstrated that IL‐20RB was expressed in A549 but absent in 293 T cells, and HSV‐1 infection did not alter this expression pattern (Figure [2G](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0002)). Notably, replenishing IL‐20RB expression in 293T cells restored IL‐19's antiviral activity (Figure [2H](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0002)).

To further validate these involved pathways, we knocked down IL‐20RB in A549 cells using small interfering RNA (siRNA) (Figure [2I](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0002)). As expected, IL‐20RB knock‐down significantly attenuated STAT3 phosphorylation and enhanced HSV‐1 replication (Figure [2J,K](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0002)). Conversely, IL‐19 overexpression promoted STAT3 phosphorylation and suppressed HSV‐1 replication (Figure [2L](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0002)). Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation with WP1066 abolished IL‐19‐mediated STAT3 activation and potentiated HSV‐1 infection (Figure [2M,N](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0002)). In addition, we found that IL‐20 and IL‐24, which also modulate IL‐20RB/STAT3 signaling pathway, exhibited similar antiviral functions (Supporting Information S1: Figure [S3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-suppl-0001)). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that IL‐19 can exert antiviral effects via IL‐20RB/STAT3 axis.

### 3.3. IL‐19 Exerts Antiviral Effects by Up‐Regulating TRIM22 {#jmv71066-sec-0210}

Next, to identify key mediators of IL‐19's antiviral activity, we analyzed transcriptomic data from IL‐19‐overexpressing cells. Among the top 10 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were TRIM22, IFITM1, IFI27, IFIT2, ISG20, LAMP3, TNFSF10, BOLA2, CASP1, IFNL1(IFN‐λ) (Figure [3A](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0003)). RT‐qPCR confirmed that IL‐19 overexpression significantly upregulated TRIM22, IFN‐λ, and other ISGs in HSV‐1‐infected A549 cells (Figure [3B--G](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0003)).

Among them, TRIM22 belongs to TRIM (Tripartite motif) protein family, which have been reported to participate in interferon signaling pathway and play an antiviral function \[[35](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0035)\]. We found that inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation significantly suppressed both TRIM22 and IFN‐λ expression (Figure [3H,I](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0003)), indicating that IL‐19 upregulates TRIM22 and IFN‐λ via IL‐20RB/STAT3 pathway. Crucially, TRIM22 overexpression in A549 cells significantly inhibited HSV‐1 replication (Figure [3J,K](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0003)) and enhanced IFN‐λ mRNA expression (Figure [3L](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0003)). These results demonstrate that IL‐19‐induced TRIM22 directly restricts viral replication.

### 3.4. IL‐19 Restricts Viral Replication but Exacerbates Pulmonary Inflammation in HSV‐1‐Infected Mice {#jmv71066-sec-0220}

To evaluate the protective efficacy of IL‐19 in vivo, C57BL/6 mice received intraperitoneal injections of IL‐19 or PBS starting from 3 days prior to HSV‐1 infection, with the injections continued for 5 consecutive days. (Figure [4A](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0004)). IL‐19 pretreatment attenuated early weight loss (Days 1--5) and significantly reduced ocular keratopathy severity at Day 5 post‐infection (Figure [4B,C](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0004)). Unexpectedly, IL‐19‐treated mice exhibited consecutive mortality from Day 6 onward, resulting in no survival benefit versus PBS controls (Figure [4D](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0004)).

To determine the reasons for this paradox, we euthanized mice from both groups that had lost 30% of their weight on Day 8 after infection. Total RNA was isolated from mouse lung and brain tissue, results showed IL‐19 pretreatment could significantly restrict HSV‐1 replication and promoted the IFN‐λ mRNA expression in mouse brain and lung (Figure [4E--H](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0004)). Moreover, the virus titer in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of the IL‐19 group was significantly lower than that of the PBS group (Figure [4I](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0004)). We also detected IFN‐λ in BALF by ELISA, and results consistently showed that IL‐19 pretreatment increased the IFN‐λ level compared to PBS pretreatment (Figure [4J](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0004)). Besides, hematoxylin‐eosin (H\&E) staining showed that the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration in the brain of the IL‐19‐pretreated group was significantly weaker than that in the PBS‐pretreated group (Figure [4O](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0004)).

Despite suppressing HSV‐1 replication, IL‐19 pretreatment failed to improve survival. To study this discrepancy, we assessed tissue‐specific inflammatory responses based on clinical associations between IL‐19 and inflammation in COVID‐19 patients. Notably, IL‐19‐pretreated mice exhibited a significant upregulation of IL‐1β and TNF‐α in the lung and brain versus PBS controls (Figure [4K--N](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0004)), and hematoxylin‐eosin (H\&E) staining also showed an increased infiltration of inflammatory cells and alveolar structural damage in IL‐19‐pretreated mice (Figure [4P](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0004)). Thus, IL‐19's failure to confer survival advantage correlates with exacerbated pulmonary inflammation. Taken together, these findings demonstrated that IL‐19 can play dual roles of both antiviral and proinflammatory activities, exemplifying the complex immunomodulatory roles of cytokines during viral infections.

{#jmv71066-sec-0230-anchor}4. Discussion {#jmv71066-sec-0230}
-------------------------------------------------------------

While multi‐omics studies have mapped IL‐19‐associated molecular events, including enzyme catalysis/post‐translational modifications, activation/inhibition events, molecular associations, gene regulations at the mRNA and protein level, and the protein translocation events \[[36](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0036)\], its role in viral infections remained underinvestigated. In this study, we revealed that IL‐19 has potential antiviral activity and its immunomodulatory function during viral infections, emphasizing the importance of cross‐talk between cytokines and antiviral immunity.

Traditionally, interleukins exert their biological function by two mechanisms: directly interacting with certain essential intracellular proteins \[[37](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0037)\], or regulating downstream functional genes via receptor‐mediated signaling pathways \[[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0012), [13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0013)\]. We herein identified IL‐20RB as the essential receptor for IL‐19's antiviral function. STATs are crucial in mediating the signaling pathway of interleukins \[[38](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0038)\] and inducing the expression of anti‐viral IFN‐stimulated genes (ISG)s \[[39](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0039)\], and we subsequently emphasized that STAT3 phosphorylation mediated by IL‐20RB played a key role in linking IL‐19 to antiviral responses. Tripartite motif (TRIM) proteins are a large family of E3 ubiquitin ligases, with an N‐terminal RING domain, one or two B‐box‐type zinc finger domains and a coiled‐coil domain \[[40](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0040)\]. Current studies have proved that TRIM proteins are involved in a variety of cellular functions, and they can act at multiple stages of the viral life cycles targeting various viral proteins \[[41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0041), [42](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0042)\]. Notably, many interleukins can regulate TRIMs. For example, IL‐22 facilitates TRIM25 to inhibit replication of DHAV‑1(duck viral hepatitis A virus type 1) \[[43](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0043)\], and IL‐27 promotes TRIM25 expression to inhibit HBV replication \[[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0012)\]. In this study, we determined that IL‐19 can upregulate TRIM22, which was recently identified as a novel restriction factor of HSV‐1 by increasing the loading of total histone H3 and heterochromatin marks \[[35](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0035)\]. However, suppressing STAT3 phosphorylation did not completely abolish the robust induction of TRIM22 protein by IL‐19, suggesting that IL‐19 may upregulate TRIM22 through alternative pathways. We also proved that TRIM22 can directly induce IFN‐λ expression to establish a feed‐forward loop, which has not been reported in previous studies. IFN‐λ is an important broad‐spectrum antiviral cytokine \[[44](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0044)\] and exerts a unique function in limiting the transmission of viruses in the respiratory tract, gastrointestinal tract, and brain \[[45](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0045)\]. Therefore, this IL‐19/IL‐20RB/STAT3/TRIM22/IFN‐λ axis represents a previously unrecognized antiviral mechanism, the other cellular and molecular interactions within this regulatory axis warrant further exploration.

In the challenge experiment, we observed that IL‐19 pretreatment effectively inhibited virus replication in mouse lung and brain with a protective effect against weight loss and symptoms of ocular herpetic keratitis in the early stage of HSV‐1 infection, but it failed to confer survival advantage in the later stage of viral infection due to exacerbated pulmonary inflammation. These findings align with clinical reports linking IL‐19 to inflammatory tissue damage and pathogenic infections \[[26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0026), [46](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0046)\] or chronic inflammation \[[47](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0047), [48](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0048), [49](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0049)\]. One possible explanation might be that the expression level of IL‐20RB in the lung tissue is significantly higher than those in brain tissue \[[50](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0050)\], and therefore IL‐19 triggered a higher expression level of IFN‐λ in the lung tissue. Notably, although IFN‐λ exhibits broad‐spectrum antiviral activity, it benefits the host mostly during early‐stage viral infection. Conversely, in later stages, IFN‐λ usually induces severe inflammatory damage in the lower respiratory tract \[[51](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0051), [52](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0052)\], reflecting the pleiotropic nature of cytokines during viral infections. Notably, some studies identify IL‐19 as an anti‐inflammatory biomarker \[[53](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0053), [54](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0054)\] and show its non‐inflammatory functions \[[55](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-bib-0055)\].

Overall, our study demonstrated that IL‐19 orchestrates antiviral immunity but exacerbates pulmonary inflammation via the IL‐20RB/STAT3/TRIM22/IFN‐λ axis (Figure [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382209/#jmv71066-fig-0005)), implying its complex immunomodulatory roles. Thus, future IL‐19‐targeted strategies must spatiotemporally control its activity to harness antiviral benefits while mitigating lung inflammation.
